# drive-post-mortem — SUCCESS

The criteria for judging whether this skill was followed. The SKILL.md is based on this file, not the other way around.

## What this verifies, and the why under it

A driven post-mortem reaches the same goal as any post-mortem — real improvement, and a corpus that models the working relationship — but through a planner conducting a handler's retro in a pane, with the SC mediated. The handler's side is marked by `post-mortem`; this marks the planner.

The planner's whole job is to economise the one scarce thing, the SC's attention, without spending the one thing that cannot be economised, the SC's judgment. It takes everything that is not the SC's — reading the pane, running the handler through the conduct, compressing, ghostwriting the SC's messages, carrying words back — and leaves the SC only the judgment: the calls, the tone, the shaping of a change.

So the failure that matters is substitution. To go faster, the planner starts standing in for the SC — making the call, agreeing a change with the handler, producing the result — and two Claudes converge on a hollow, mutually agreeable file: the thing that looks done and improves nothing. Marking the planner bets that a conductor who kept the SC central produced a real retro, and one that stood in produced theatre.

A marking guide based on evidence. No post-mortem was driven, nothing to mark — see N/A.

## Where to look

The planner's session — what it brought the SC, and the messages it sent the handler as the SC — alongside the handler's pane and the post-mortem the retro produced. The handler's conduct is `post-mortem`'s to judge; here you read the planner: what it surfaced, what it sent, and where the decisions actually came from.

## How to judge

Weigh the planner against the two things at once: did it keep the SC central, and did it lift the load around the SC off them. A planner that did neither is a clerk; one that did the second by doing the first's job for it is the failure. The examples are real shapes, not strings to match.

### PASS

- **Kept the SC at the decisions, did not stand in.** Every call, every shaping of a change, every "is this right" was the SC's, surfaced by the planner and carried back. The planner did not make a call, settle a change with the handler, or decide anything itself and present it done.
  - *Good:* the handler hit a type-vs-message call; the planner brought the SC what it turned on and carried the SC's answer to the handler.
  - *Bad:* the planner told the handler "go with message," or agreed the change with the handler and showed the SC a finished file to approve.
- **Compressed, did not dump or hide.** The planner read the pane and brought the SC the decision and what it turns on, not the raw transcript (a wall to sort) and not silence (the thing buried). The one-minute test: the SC could act on what was brought, in the time they have.
  - *Good:* "PASS on the fix, but the supervisor never checked the comment you care about — that's the one call for you."
  - *Bad:* pasting the full pane up to the SC, or sending nothing and writing the file.
- **Brought its own read, did not parrot.** A handler's claim or a supervisor's verdict reached the SC as the planner's checked judgment of it, not relayed.
  - *Good:* "the supervisor passed it; CI is green and the diff matches, but it verified the build, not whether it is what you wanted."
  - *Bad:* "It says PASS."
- **Spoke to the handler in the SC's voice, intact.** The messages sent to the handler as the SC carried the SC's actual input, in the SC's voice, not a paraphrase that drifted what the SC meant.
  - *Good:* the message reads as the SC, direct, their words and their decision.
  - *Bad:* the SC's point softened, reshaped, or pulled into the planner's register before it reached the handler.
- **Held the handler to the conduct.** The planner ran the handler through the `post-mortem` conduct — the two phases, identification only, surface not decide — rather than letting it jump or sprawl.
  - *Good:* the handler began answering "what didn't go well" with a fix; the planner pulled it back to identification.
  - *Bad:* the planner let the handler write the file and recommend changes unchecked.

### FAIL — judgment, read across the planner's messages and the outcome

The retro fails where the decisions came from the planner, not the SC: a call made and presented done, a change agreed with the handler before the SC saw it, the planner and handler converging on a file the SC only rubber-stamped. It fails where the planner dumped the raw pane on the SC, or hid what the SC needed; where it relayed a verdict or a claim without its own read; where the SC's words were paraphrased into drift before they reached the handler. A driven retro where the SC's part was approval, not judgment, is the central failure: the planner stood in, and the file is the two-Claude hollow.

Judgments, not counts. The single test: were the decisions genuinely the SC's, carried by the planner — or did the planner stand in for the SC and hand them a result to nod at?
