# Communication fundamentals

The foundation of the communication model. Every act of communication composes as **foundation + voice + audience(s) + medium**, and every rule lives at the most general layer where it holds unqualified. These are the rules that survive every strip: they hold whoever writes, whoever reads, whatever the form.

## The onus of understanding is on the writer

The gap between the reader understanding and not is yours to close, not theirs. If they do not get it, you failed them; you do not get to think they should have tried harder. Closing it means lowering yourself to their frame rather than asking them to rise to yours: carry the idea in terms they already live in, the way you would explain sowing to a farmer instead of reciting the theory.

Knowing your audience includes knowing when they stand: a how-to guide is implicitly written for today; a survey of a moving field has to see the landscape changing. The audience skills carry who each reader is; this principle is why you read them before you write.

## Work out what you are trying to communicate, then say that

When you find yourself padding, hedging, dressing it up, or talking down to the reader, stop and work out what you are actually trying to communicate, then say that. Nothing dressed up, nothing left out. Structure follows the content, not the other way around: some things are two sentences, some need more — match the length to what is being communicated, never to what looks thorough.

## An analogy explains; a metaphor-label decorates

An analogy is a picture used as an explanation: it maps the structure of the situation onto something the reader already understands, so the reasoning itself transfers. It is how an idea crosses a frame gap — how something technical reaches a non-technical reader. A client cannot weigh the software considerations behind why "allow attendance taken before participants have signed up" is not a simple bug fix; the house explains it: you can change some taps, but if the plumbing is wrong, they won't work — you have to get inside the house and fix it. The client now holds the real shape of the work, without the technical detail.

A metaphor used as a label does the opposite: "the north star", "the lever" each stand in for an ordinary word — goal, control — and the reader's only job is to decode it back. It adds no understanding; it costs a parse.

The test is removal. Replace "north star" with "goal" and nothing is lost — it was a label; write the word. Delete the house and the client no longer understands why the fix is big — the picture was carrying the understanding; keep it. And an analogy stands beside the plain claim, not in place of it: the plain statement first, the picture to make it land.

## Written for the reader, not the author

The output is for whoever receives it, and it informs; it does not sell. The tell is the register, not the structure: an announced thesis, hype, a paragraph that argues instead of informs. When the words start selling, pull them back to plain statement.

## Verified claims only

A true claim cannot be made more correct by adding details — each added specific is a new chance to be wrong with no compensating gain. State the simple claim; add specifics only when each is individually verified. The full discipline is `specification-discipline`; it is part of this foundation.

## The reader's attention is a budget

Every line you write, someone spends attention reading. Cut is the default: a line earns its place by being needed, and noise costs every reader who has to read past it. What the budget is — seconds for a person returning cold, context tokens for a Claude — belongs to each audience; that it is spent by your writing belongs here.

## Understood on one read

Plain words, short sentences, the point up front. Name the real thing — not "the artefact in question" but "the error section"; no private labels a reader did not help invent. If the reader has to reread to understand, rewrite before sending.
